The 21-Year-Old Turning Plastic Into Fuel Took the Stage at Invest Fest

Julian Brown built a solar-powered reactor in a backyard to turn plastic waste into fuel. This weekend his work was part of the conversation at Invest Fest 2026 in Atlanta. The story is bigger than the machine.

A Near Future Editorial

August 9, 2026

17

The age Brown was when he started building his plastic-to-fuel reactor

Every year Invest Fest fills the Georgia World Congress Center with the loudest names in wealth, business, and culture. This year one of the more quietly compelling stories in the room belonged to someone most of the crowd had only seen on a phone screen: Julian Brown, 21, the founder of NatureJAB, a self-taught builder who has spent years trying to turn plastic waste back into usable fuel.

Brown's process is called microwave pyrolysis. In plain terms, he heats shredded plastic in a sealed, oxygen-starved chamber until it breaks down into a vapor, then condenses that vapor into a crude liquid he calls Plastoline, which can in theory be refined toward gasoline, diesel, or jet fuel. What makes his version his own is the setup: he powers the reactor with solar panels and has iterated on it, publicly and messily, since he was a teenager.

The spark was almost cartoonishly simple. In high school he watched a video of a sea turtle with a straw lodged in its nose and could not shake the size of the plastic problem against how few real solutions existed. So he started building, in a backyard, with parts he could get his hands on.

"What motivated me was seeing the large plastic issue at hand and not seeing any solution effective enough to make a difference."

Here is where ANF stays honest, because the internet has not always been. Brown did not invent pyrolysis. Turning plastic into fuel through heat is a well-understood chemical process that industrial engineers have worked on for decades, and fact-checkers have pushed back on some of the bigger claims that trailed his videos online. There are real, unresolved questions about efficiency, scale, and whether backyard pyrolysis can ever be clean or economical. At one point Brown was hospitalized with burns after an accident with the system.

None of that is the reason to write him off. It is the reason to pay attention. A 21-year-old with no lab, no corporate backing, and no permission built something real enough to argue about, took the burns, kept iterating, and ended up on a stage in front of tens of thousands of people. The lesson is not "he solved plastic." The lesson is the posture.

That is the exact profile A Near Future exists to amplify: young, curious, technically fearless, and unwilling to wait until an institution says the idea is allowed. Detroit is full of that same energy, sitting in bedrooms and garages, one encouraging room away from being taken seriously.

Invest Fest gave Brown that room. The near future belongs to the builders who keep going after the first explosion, literal or otherwise.
