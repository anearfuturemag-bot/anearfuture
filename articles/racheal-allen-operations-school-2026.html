Detroit Has Plenty of Startup Classes. She Built the One on Staying Alive.

Racheal Allen noticed everyone was teaching Detroit how to start a business and almost no one was teaching how to run one. Operations School has spent six years closing that gap. Now it has $2.8 million to scale.

A Near Future Editorial

September 2026

2,000

Detroit founders and nonprofit leaders trained since 2019.

Racheal Allen grew up watching the hard version of entrepreneurship. On the northeast side of Detroit, she saw her mother leave a steady job to serve other small business owners while raising four children alone. Allen associated that instability with owning a business and swore she would never do it. Her plan was a car she could count on, a house she would never leave, and a paycheck every two weeks.

She became a mother at seventeen, earned a full scholarship to Marygrove College, and launched her first company, a Farmers Insurance agency, at twenty-one. That was where the fear turned into a calling. Nearly twenty years and several ventures later, she has built the thing Detroit founders quietly needed most.

The idea arrived in a classroom. Sitting in a course at the BUILD Institute in 2017, Allen realized something the city had a blind spot for. Detroit was full of programs teaching people how to start a business. Almost none were teaching people how to operate one. The systems, the standard procedures, the back-office machinery that decides whether a business is still standing in year three. In 2019 she launched Operations School to fill exactly that gap.

"Plenty of people will teach you how to start. She teaches you how to survive."

Operations School, known to its people as OSchool, runs on a flagship program called Get Your Business Legit that walks founders through legal registration, operations, marketing, and long-term planning. It has grown from coaching into a full done-for-you agency, staffed by women practitioners who run their own micro-businesses and handle project management, business development, and client services for startups and nonprofits. Since 2019 it has trained close to 2,000 entrepreneurs and nonprofit leaders, and Allen has funneled well over 200,000 dollars of her own money into the work.

In 2022 she co-founded Centric Place, a hub for arts, culture, and entrepreneurship that supports more than 1,000 Black-owned businesses a year and operates as one of 27 Small Business Support Hubs backed by the Michigan Economic Development Corporation. This year Operations School was awarded a 2.8 million dollar grant to expand its programming and go deeper with the founders it serves. By the Michigan Chronicle's account, Allen was the only Black woman among the 27 organizations selected.

$2.8M

Grant awarded to Operations School to scale its programming for Detroit founders.

The money is a headline. The model is the story. Grants and funds get announced in Detroit almost every week, and most of the attention flows to the founders raising rounds. Allen built the layer underneath all of it, the unglamorous operational spine that keeps a promising business from quietly folding once the launch energy fades. When she wins, hundreds of businesses she will never be publicly credited for get a little more durable.

This is the builder ANF exists to spotlight. Not the loudest raise, but the person constructing the on-ramp everyone else drives up. Allen turned the instability she grew up fearing into the exact infrastructure that protects the next founder from it.

She almost avoided this life entirely. Instead she built the school Detroit's founders never had, and handed them the part nobody else was teaching.
