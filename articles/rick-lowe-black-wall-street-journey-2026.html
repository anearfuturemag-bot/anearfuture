He Turned a Stock Ticker Toward Black Wealth.

Rick Lowe, the MacArthur artist who rebuilt a Houston neighborhood house by house, opens his largest US museum show at the University of Michigan, an hour west of Detroit. Black Wall Street Journey borrows the language of finance and points it at the parts of Black life the market never counts.

A Near Future Editorial

September 2026

1921

The year Tulsa's Greenwood, the original Black Wall Street, was burned to the ground. Lowe's project answers it.

A stock ticker is a machine for telling you what a thing is worth. It runs in green and red across the bottom of a screen, and everything it names has a price. Rick Lowe took that form, the most literal symbol of the market there is, and pointed it at the things a market never bothers to count. Trust between neighbors. Whether a corner feels safe at night. What a block is actually worth to the people who live on it.

Lowe is not a household name the way a pop artist is, but he is one of the most consequential builders in American art. In 1993 he took twenty-two derelict shotgun houses in Houston's Third Ward and turned them into Project Row Houses, a work of art that is also a functioning neighborhood, now spread across five city blocks. In 2014 he was named a MacArthur Fellow. His whole practice runs on a simple refusal: that art belongs on a wall, separate from the life around it. He calls the alternative social sculpture, and the material is the community itself.

Now that practice comes to Michigan. Black Wall Street Journey: Re and De Imagining Black Progress opens September 26 at the University of Michigan Museum of Art in Ann Arbor and runs through late January. UMMA is calling it Lowe's largest solo museum exhibition in the United States, built from roughly twenty new works inside the galleries and a large LED installation that carries the piece up onto the roof, where the ticker becomes something the whole campus can see.

He asked a Black neighborhood to define its own wealth, then put the answer where a ticker usually goes.

The name reaches back to Greenwood, the Tulsa district known as Black Wall Street until a white mob destroyed it in 1921. Lowe began the project in 2018 as a way to understand the economic and social health of a Black neighborhood from the inside, from residents rather than analysts, and then to project that understanding in sculptural form. The ticker is the tell. It quotes a different index, one built from what people say matters where they live.

The Michigan show did not arrive out of nowhere. Lowe spent two years as an artist in residence with the University's Institute for Social Research through the Arts Initiative, working alongside students and researchers who spend their careers measuring exactly this kind of thing. A social scientist counts a neighborhood in numbers. An artist asks what the numbers leave out. Putting the two in the same room for two years is the actual work, and the exhibition is what came out of it.

This is the ANF Take. We spend a lot of energy at ANF on the dollar figures, the raises, the grants, the pitch nights, because those are real and they matter. Lowe is a reminder of the other ledger. A neighborhood can be rich in the things a spreadsheet cannot hold and broke in the things it can, and progress that only ever counts the second column keeps missing the point. An artist who spent thirty years building actual houses has more standing than most to say that, and he is saying it in the plainest language capitalism has, its own scoreboard.

Ann Arbor is not Detroit, and this is not a Detroit story in the strict sense. It is a Michigan one, close enough to reach, and it belongs to the same conversation this magazine keeps returning to. What does it mean to build something that stays and that the community can actually reach. Lowe answered that question in Houston with a hammer and twenty-two houses. Thirty years on, he is answering it again with a ticker that quotes a number Wall Street never learned to read. Worth the drive.
