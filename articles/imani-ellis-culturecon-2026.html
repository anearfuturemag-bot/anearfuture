The Weekend Always Ends. She Built Something That Doesn't.

Imani Ellis grew CultureCon from a living-room meetup into a 12,000-person movement for Black and Brown creatives. In 2026 she launched CultureCon Collective to keep the room open after everyone goes home.

A Near Future Editorial

September 2026

12,000

People CultureCon drew in a single weekend last year, nine years after it started in her living room.

Imani Ellis moved to New York not knowing a single person. She had been a ballerina, then a college track athlete on scholarship at Vanderbilt, then an NBC page at 30 Rockefeller Plaza who got pulled into a communications job at Bravo after two months. What she did not have was a room of people who understood what she was trying to become.

So in 2016 she built one. The Creative Collective started as a gathering for Black and Brown creatives in her Harlem living room. It outgrew the apartment fast. In 2017 she turned the idea into CultureCon, a conference where Black and Brown voices could collaborate and celebrate each other instead of code-switching through somebody else's event.

The scale that followed is the part people quote. Guests like Tracee Ellis Ross and Regina King. Brand partners like Target, Google, HBO, and Netflix. A 2026 New York lineup carrying John Legend, Mahershala Ali, Niecy Nash-Betts, and Sherri Shepherd. Last year the festival drew 12,000 people through the door.

"Our community is growing up, their businesses are expanding, their ambitions are bigger, and CultureCon has to grow with them."

But the number Ellis kept coming back to was not attendance. It was what happened on Monday. Her community kept asking for connection that lasted longer than a weekend. So in 2026 she launched CultureCon Collective, a year-round platform built to keep the room open after everyone goes home.

That is a different kind of decision than throwing a bigger party. Ellis rebuilt the thing around what her people actually said they needed, which was to keep learning and keep building, not just to show up once a year and feel seen for forty-eight hours.

This is the ANF Take. A weekend can change how you feel. Infrastructure changes what you can do. Ellis did not just grow an event, she turned a feeling into a company, then turned the company into something that shows up all year. That is the whole difference between a moment and a movement.

Detroit knows this lesson in its bones. This city runs on one-night functions, pop-ups, and cookouts that light up a block and vanish by morning. The builders worth watching here are the ones asking the harder question Ellis already answered. What do we keep after the crowd leaves.

The party always ends. The people who build something that outlasts it are the ones who were never really throwing a party to begin with.
