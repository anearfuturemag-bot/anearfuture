She Left Detroit With a Suitcase. She Came Back to Open the Door.

Rose Vitale left metro Detroit at 26 and built a wireless-retail empire in California. In 2026 she came home, putting $1 million into a Royal Oak home for women founders.

A Near Future Editorial

September 2026

$1M

What Vitale is putting into a single 9,000-square-foot building in Royal Oak, built for women founders.

Rose Vitale left metro Detroit at 26 with little more than a suitcase and a decision to bet on herself. She landed in California and, two years later, in the middle of the 2008 financial crisis and against most of the advice she was getting, she expanded her wireless retail operations instead of pulling back.

The gamble held. Vitale grew into the largest retail partner of Cricket Wireless, then a subsidiary of Leap Wireless. Her footprint shifted the market enough that when AT&T acquired Leap in 2014, she was already operating at a scale most people never reach. She spent the years after that as a serial entrepreneur and investor inside the California ecosystem that taught her how founders actually get funded.

Then she did the thing Detroit talent almost never does. She came back.

The talent was never the gap. The room was.

In May 2024 Vitale founded the Female Founder Institute, a nonprofit built to give women entrepreneurs the coworking space, coaching, and capital-building education she had to leave the state to find. In 2026 she is planting its first physical hub in a 9,000-square-foot building on Main Street in Royal Oak, investing roughly $1 million into the space itself.

Royal Oak was a deliberate pick. Vitale called it a central, open-minded place inside metro Detroit that reminded her of the culture she found on the coast. The Institute runs on a membership model: access to the coworking floor, the coaching, and the classes. She has already launched a $100,000 pitch competition that took applications from founders across the country, and a second location in San Francisco is in the works.

This is the ANF Take. Detroit is very good at producing ambitious people and very bad at keeping them. The city exports its founders to the coasts, watches them win, and rarely sees the return trip. Vitale ran that whole arc, all the way to an AT&T acquisition, and then chose to point the infrastructure back home. She is not opening a coworking space. She is building the room she needed at 26 and could not find here.

The part worth watching is who it is for. A hub aimed specifically at women founders, in a region where access to capital and rooms full of the right people has never been evenly handed out, is not a nice amenity. It is a direct answer to the reason a lot of talent leaves in the first place.

Detroit does not need more people to make it out. It needs more of the ones who did to come back and hold the door. Vitale just showed what that looks like in square footage.
