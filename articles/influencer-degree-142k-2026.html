College Just Turned the Influencer Dream Into a $142K Degree

Arizona State now offers a bachelor's in content creation. Syracuse adds a creator minor this fall. The job your parents warned you about now comes with a diploma and a tuition bill.

A Near Future Editorial

August 2026

$142K

Estimated cost of Arizona State's new bachelor's in content creation

For years, telling your family you wanted to be an influencer got you a lecture. Now it can get you a college major. Arizona State University has launched a bachelor's in content creation through its Walter Cronkite School of Journalism and Mass Communication, a four-year degree built to teach students how to grow a brand and make impactful posts across video, podcast, and social platforms. By some estimates the full program can run around 142,000 dollars.

ASU is not alone. This fall Syracuse University is launching an interdisciplinary minor designed to prepare students for careers in content creation, digital entrepreneurship, and the broader ecosystem that supports creators and online businesses. The message from higher education is clear: the creator path is real enough to sell a curriculum around.

"The internet's side hustle just became a credential. The question is whether the credential builds the audience, or just the debt."

The demand is not made up. More than half of Gen Z say they want to become influencers, and Goldman Sachs projects the creator economy could approach 500 billion dollars by 2027, with more than 50 million creators worldwide. Universities watched an entire generation choose the phone over the cubicle, and they moved to put a price tag on the dream. ASU frames the degree as a runway to lucrative corporate roles too: marketing associate around 133,000 dollars, marketing manager around 166,000 dollars, PR manager around 147,000 dollars.

Here is the part that deserves scrutiny. The people who actually built audiences over the last decade did not do it in a lecture hall. They did it by posting, failing, posting again, and learning the platform in public. A 2025 creator report found that 78 percent of creators say burnout is affecting their motivation and their health, and declining consumer spending has made brand deals less predictable. Those are the real lessons of the work, and none of them show up neatly on a syllabus.

This is where ANF sits on it. A diploma does not build an audience. Reps do. If a young creator in Detroit has 142,000 dollars and four years, the fastest education is a camera, a consistent posting schedule, and a real point of view, not a tuition invoice for a skill the platform will teach you for free. The degree can be a fine choice for some. It should never be the barrier that decides who gets to build.

The creator economy did not need permission to become the new American dream. Watching a university try to sell it back to the same kids who invented it should tell you exactly how valuable the thing you already have access to really is.
